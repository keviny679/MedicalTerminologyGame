## Muscles and Tendons
@source 97-99 | Textbook section 5.1, pp. 97-99
> The [[muscular system]] moves the body, holds posture and makes heat. There are three kinds of muscle.
> [[skeletal muscle]] attaches to bones and is a [[voluntary muscle]], under your control. [[smooth muscle]]
> lines organs and blood vessels and is an [[involuntary muscle]]. [[cardiac muscle]] is found only in the heart
> and is also involuntary. Skeletal and cardiac muscle are both [[striated muscle]], striped under a microscope.
> Each long muscle cell is a [[muscle fiber]], and muscles are wrapped and separated by sheets of [[fascia]]. A
> muscle pulls on bone through a [[tendon]]; a broad, flat tendon is an [[aponeurosis]]. A muscle's [[origin]]
> is where it attaches to the bone that stays still, and its [[insertion]] is where it attaches to the bone that
> moves. Movement comes from [[contraction]], and even at rest a muscle keeps a little tension called [[muscle
> tone]].
T muscular system = the muscles of the body, which produce movement, maintain posture and generate heat
T skeletal muscle = voluntary striated muscle attached to bones that moves the body | @joint.muscle
T smooth muscle = involuntary muscle in the walls of organs and blood vessels
T cardiac muscle = involuntary striated muscle found only in the heart
T striated muscle = muscle that looks striped under a microscope; skeletal and cardiac muscle
T voluntary muscle = muscle under conscious control, such as skeletal muscle
T involuntary muscle = muscle that works without conscious control, such as smooth and cardiac muscle
T muscle fiber = a single long muscle cell
T fascia = a sheet of fibrous tissue that surrounds and separates muscles | fasci/o + -a
T tendon = a tough cord of fibrous tissue that connects muscle to bone | @joint.tendon
T aponeurosis = a flat, sheet-like tendon that attaches a muscle over a wide area
T origin = the attachment of a muscle to the bone that stays still
T insertion = the attachment of a muscle to the bone that moves
T muscle tone = the slight, constant tension of a resting muscle
T contraction = shortening of a muscle to produce movement
P my/o, muscul/o = muscle
P fasci/o = fascia
P rhabd/o = rod-shaped, striated
P leiomy/o = smooth muscle
P -trophy = development, nourishment

> Joint movements come in opposite pairs. [[flexion]] bends a joint and decreases its angle, as when you bend
> the elbow, while [[extension]] straightens it; pushing past straight is [[hyperextension]]. [[abduction]]
> moves a limb away from the midline of the body, and [[adduction]] moves it back toward the midline.
> [[rotation]] turns a bone around its own axis, and [[circumduction]] swings a limb in a circle. Turning the
> palm down is [[pronation]], and turning it up is [[supination]]. At the ankle, pulling the toes up is
> [[dorsiflexion]] and pointing them down is [[plantar flexion]], while turning the sole inward is [[inversion]]
> and outward is [[eversion]]. How far a joint can move is its [[range of motion]]. Muscles work in teams: the
> [[agonist]] produces a movement while its [[antagonist]] relaxes; for bending the elbow, the biceps is the
> agonist and the triceps is the antagonist.
T flexion = bending a joint to decrease the angle between bones
T extension = straightening a joint to increase the angle between bones
T hyperextension = extending a joint beyond its normal straight position | hyper- + extension
T abduction = moving a limb away from the midline of the body | ab- + duct/o + -ion
T adduction = moving a limb toward the midline of the body | ad- + duct/o + -ion
T rotation = turning a bone around its own axis
T circumduction = moving a limb in a circle | circum- + duct/o + -ion
T pronation = turning the palm downward or backward
T supination = turning the palm upward or forward
T dorsiflexion = bending the foot upward toward the shin | dors/o + flexion
T plantar flexion = pointing the foot downward
T inversion = turning the sole of the foot inward
T eversion = turning the sole of the foot outward
T range of motion = how far a joint can move in each direction
T agonist = the main muscle that produces a movement; also called the prime mover
T antagonist = the muscle that opposes a movement and relaxes while the agonist contracts
P duct/o = to lead
P kinesi/o = movement
P -kinesia = movement
P circum- = around


## Muscles and Tendons of the Upper and Lower Extremities
@source 100-103 | Textbook section 5.2, pp. 100-103
> Many muscles are named for their shape, location or action. The [[deltoid]] is the triangle-shaped muscle
> capping the shoulder and a common injection site. On the upper arm, the [[biceps brachii]] bends the elbow and
> the [[triceps brachii]] straightens it. The [[pectoralis major]] covers the chest, the [[trapezius]] spans the
> upper back and neck, and the [[latissimus dorsi]] is the broad muscle of the middle back. In the neck, the
> [[sternocleidomastoid]] runs from behind the ear to the sternum and clavicle and turns the head. The [[rectus
> abdominis]] runs vertically down the abdomen, with the [[external oblique]] on the sides. The [[gluteus
> maximus]] is the large buttock muscle. On the thigh, the [[quadriceps]] in front straighten the knee and the
> [[hamstrings]] behind bend it, and the [[gastrocnemius]] is the calf muscle. The [[rotator cuff]] holds the
> shoulder joint stable, and the [[diaphragm]] is the main muscle of breathing.
T deltoid = the triangular muscle covering the shoulder, a common injection site
T biceps brachii = the muscle on the front of the upper arm that bends the elbow
T triceps brachii = the muscle on the back of the upper arm that straightens the elbow
T pectoralis major = the large fan-shaped chest muscle that moves the arm across the body
T trapezius = the large diamond-shaped muscle of the upper back and neck that moves the shoulder blade
T latissimus dorsi = the broad muscle of the middle and lower back that pulls the arm down and back
T sternocleidomastoid = the neck muscle running from behind the ear to the sternum and clavicle that turns the head
T rectus abdominis = the vertical abdominal muscle that bends the trunk forward
T external oblique = the outer side muscle of the abdomen that twists and bends the trunk
T gluteus maximus = the large buttock muscle that extends the hip
T quadriceps = the group of four muscles on the front of the thigh that extend the knee
T hamstrings = the three muscles at the back of the thigh that bend the knee
T gastrocnemius = the calf muscle that points the foot downward
T rotator cuff = the four muscles and their tendons that hold the shoulder joint stable
T diaphragm = the dome-shaped muscle under the lungs that is the main muscle of breathing


## Disorders and Injuries of Muscles and Tendons
@source 104-106 | Textbook section 5.3, pp. 104-106
> The largest tendon in the body is the [[Achilles tendon]], which joins the calf muscles to the heel bone. A
> [[strain]] is a stretched or torn muscle or tendon, while a complete tear is a [[tendon rupture]]. Overuse
> causes [[tendinitis]], inflammation of a tendon, or [[tenosynovitis]] when the sheath around the tendon is
> inflamed too. A [[rotator cuff tear]] causes shoulder pain and weakness when lifting the arm. [[lateral
> epicondylitis]], or tennis elbow, is painful inflammation of the tendons on the outer elbow. [[plantar
> fasciitis]] inflames the thick band of fascia on the sole and causes heel pain, worst with the first steps in
> the morning. In [[carpal tunnel syndrome]], swollen tendons in the wrist squeeze the median nerve, causing
> numbness in the fingers. A [[ganglion cyst]] is a fluid-filled lump on a tendon sheath, often on the wrist,
> and [[shin splints]] are pain along the shinbone from overworked muscles and tendons.
T Achilles tendon = the tendon joining the calf muscles to the heel bone
T strain = a stretched or torn muscle or tendon
T tendinitis = inflammation of a tendon | tendin/o + -itis
T tenosynovitis = inflammation of a tendon and the sheath around it | ten/o + synov/o + -itis
T tendon rupture = a complete tear of a tendon
T rotator cuff tear = a tear in one or more rotator cuff tendons, causing shoulder pain and weakness
T lateral epicondylitis = painful inflammation of the tendons on the outer elbow; tennis elbow | epi- + condyl/o + -itis
T plantar fasciitis = inflammation of the thick band of fascia on the sole of the foot, causing heel pain | fasci/o + -itis
T carpal tunnel syndrome = numbness and pain in the hand from pressure on the median nerve at the wrist
T ganglion cyst = a fluid-filled lump that forms on a tendon sheath or joint, often on the wrist
T shin splints = pain along the shinbone from overworked muscles and tendons
P ten/o, tend/o, tendin/o = tendon
P condyl/o = knuckle, rounded end of a bone

> Muscle pain is [[myalgia]], and any disease of muscle is a [[myopathy]]. Inflammation of muscle is
> [[myositis]]; when it is autoimmune and affects many muscles it is [[polymyositis]]. [[fibromyalgia]] is a
> chronic condition of widespread muscle pain and tenderness with fatigue. [[muscular dystrophy]] is a group of
> inherited diseases in which muscle steadily weakens and wastes away. [[myasthenia gravis]] is an autoimmune
> disease that blocks nerve signals to muscle, causing weakness that worsens with use. [[rhabdomyolysis]] is a
> rapid breakdown of skeletal muscle that releases proteins that can damage the kidneys. Muscle wasting from
> disuse is [[atrophy of muscle]], muscle growth from exercise is [[hypertrophy of muscle]], and the loss of
> muscle with aging is [[sarcopenia]]. A [[spasm]] is a sudden involuntary contraction, and a painful sustained
> one is a [[cramp]]. Permanent shortening that limits movement is a [[contracture]], and dangerous pressure
> inside a closed group of muscles is [[compartment syndrome]].
T myalgia = muscle pain | my/o + -algia
T myopathy = any disease of muscle | my/o + -pathy
T myositis = inflammation of muscle | my/o + -itis
T polymyositis = an autoimmune inflammation of many muscles causing weakness | poly- + my/o + -itis
T fibromyalgia = a chronic condition of widespread muscle pain, tenderness and fatigue | fibr/o + my/o + -algia
T muscular dystrophy = an inherited group of diseases causing progressive muscle weakness and wasting | dys- + -trophy
T myasthenia gravis = an autoimmune disease causing muscle weakness and fatigue | my/o + -asthenia
T rhabdomyolysis = breakdown of skeletal muscle that releases harmful proteins into the blood | rhabd/o + my/o + -lysis
T atrophy of muscle = wasting away of muscle from disuse or disease | a- + -trophy
T hypertrophy of muscle = enlargement of muscle from exercise or extra work | hyper- + -trophy
T sarcopenia = loss of muscle mass and strength with aging | sarc/o + -penia
T spasm = a sudden involuntary contraction of a muscle
T cramp = a painful, sustained muscle spasm
T contracture = permanent shortening of a muscle or tendon that limits joint movement
T compartment syndrome = dangerous pressure buildup inside a closed group of muscles that cuts off blood flow
T leiomyoma = a benign tumor of smooth muscle | leiomy/o + -oma
T rhabdomyosarcoma = a malignant tumor of skeletal muscle | rhabd/o + my/o + sarc/o + -oma
P fibr/o = fiber
P -asthenia = weakness
P sarc/o = flesh, connective tissue


## Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation (PM&R)
@source 107-109 | Textbook section 5.4, pp. 107-109
> [[physical medicine and rehabilitation]] restores function and quality of life after illness or injury. A [[physiatrist]] directs rehabilitation care, [[physical therapy]] improves movement, and [[occupational therapy]] builds skills needed for daily activities. A [[prosthesis]] replaces a missing body part.
T physical medicine and rehabilitation = specialty focused on restoring function and quality of life after illness or injury
T physiatrist = physician specializing in physical medicine and rehabilitation
T physical therapy = treatment using exercise and physical methods to restore movement and function
T occupational therapy = treatment that develops or restores skills for daily living and work
T prosthesis = artificial replacement for a missing body part

## Procedures and Pharmacology
@source 110-114 | Textbook section 5.5, pp. 110-114
> Muscle problems are tested with [[electromyography]], which records the electrical activity of muscles, and a
> [[muscle biopsy]], which removes a small piece for examination. A blood test for [[creatine kinase]] shows
> muscle damage, since this enzyme leaks out of injured muscle. The specialty of [[physical medicine and
> rehabilitation]] restores function after injury or illness, and its doctor is a [[physiatrist]]. [[physical
> therapy]] rebuilds strength and movement with exercise, while [[occupational therapy]] helps people relearn
> the skills of daily life and work. A [[muscle relaxant]] relieves spasms. Surgery uses the word parts you
> know: [[tenorrhaphy]] sutures a torn tendon, [[tenotomy]] cuts a tendon, [[myorrhaphy]] sutures a muscle, and
> [[fasciotomy]] cuts fascia to relieve the pressure of compartment syndrome.
T electromyography = recording the electrical activity of muscles | electr/o + my/o + -graphy
T muscle biopsy = removal of a small piece of muscle for examination
T creatine kinase = an enzyme released into the blood when muscle is damaged
T muscle relaxant = a drug that relieves muscle spasms
T tenorrhaphy = surgical suturing of a tendon | ten/o + -rrhaphy
T tenotomy = surgical cutting of a tendon | ten/o + -otomy
T fasciotomy = surgical cutting of fascia to relieve pressure | fasci/o + -otomy
T myorrhaphy = surgical suturing of a muscle | my/o + -rrhaphy
P electr/o = electricity
P physi/o = nature, physical function

A ROM = range of motion
A EMG = electromyography
A CK = creatine kinase
A CTS = carpal tunnel syndrome
A PT = physical therapy
A OT = occupational therapy
A PM&R = physical medicine and rehabilitation
A RICE = rest, ice, compression, elevation
A MG = myasthenia gravis
A DMD = Duchenne muscular dystrophy
