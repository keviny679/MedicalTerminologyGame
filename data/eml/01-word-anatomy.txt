## Building medical words
> Most medical terms are built from a small set of parts, so learning the parts lets you decode thousands of
> words. The [[root]] carries the core meaning, such as cardi meaning heart. A [[combining vowel]], usually o,
> links a root to another part, and a root plus its vowel is written as a [[combining form]], like cardi/o. A
> [[prefix]] sits at the front of a word and changes its meaning, while a [[suffix]] completes the word at the
> end. Breaking a term into these pieces is called [[word analysis]], and the usual way to define a term is to
> start with the suffix, then the prefix, then the root. Clinical words describe disease too. A [[sign]] is
> something objective you can observe or measure, like a fever, while a [[symptom]] is something the patient
> feels and reports, like pain. Identifying the disease is the [[diagnosis]], and predicting its course is the
> [[prognosis]]. An [[acute]] illness starts suddenly and is short, while a [[chronic]] one lasts a long time.
> The cause of a disease is its [[etiology]]; a disease with no known cause is [[idiopathic]], and a problem
> caused by treatment is [[iatrogenic]].
T root = the core part of a medical word that carries its main meaning
T combining vowel = a vowel (usually o) added after a root to link it to another word part
T combining form = a root plus its combining vowel, written with a slash, such as cardi/o
T prefix = a word part placed before a root to change its meaning
T suffix = a word part placed at the end of a word to change or complete its meaning
T word analysis = breaking a medical term into its parts to work out its meaning
T definition by parts = defining a term by starting with the suffix, then the prefix, then the root
T eponym = a disease, structure or procedure named after a person, such as Alzheimer disease
T acronym = an abbreviation formed from first letters and pronounced as a word, such as laser
T abbreviation = a shortened form of a word or phrase, such as BP for blood pressure
T homonym = a word that sounds like another word but has a different meaning, such as ileum and ilium
T diagnosis = identifying a disease from its signs, symptoms and tests | dia- + gnos/o + -sis
T prognosis = the predicted outcome or course of a disease | pro- + gnos/o + -sis
T sign = objective evidence of disease that can be observed or measured, such as a fever
T symptom = a subjective sensation of disease that the patient reports, such as pain
T syndrome = a set of signs and symptoms that occur together and point to a particular condition
T acute = describes a condition with sudden onset and short duration
T chronic = describes a condition that persists for a long time or keeps coming back
T etiology = the study or the cause of a disease | eti/o + -logy
T idiopathic = describes a disease of unknown cause | idi/o + path/o + -ic
T iatrogenic = describes a problem caused by medical treatment | iatr/o + gen/o + -ic
T nosocomial = describes an infection acquired in a hospital or health care setting
T pathology = the study of disease | path/o + -logy
T physiology = the study of how the body and its parts function | physi/o + -logy
T anatomy = the study of the structure of the body

## Common prefixes
> Prefixes tell you about number, position, time, speed or amount. Position prefixes are everywhere:
> [[endo-|endo-]] means within, [[epi-]] means upon, [[sub-|sub-, infra-]] means below, [[inter-]] means
> between, [[intra-]] means within, and [[peri-]] means around. For amount, [[hyper-]] means above normal and
> [[hypo-]] means below normal, so hypertension and hypotension are opposites. Speed prefixes appear in heart
> and breathing words: [[brady-]] means slow and [[tachy-]] means fast. [[dys-]] means bad, painful or
> difficult, while [[eu-]] means good or normal. Many prefixes deal with number: [[mono-|mono-, uni-]] is one,
> [[bi-|bi-, di-]] is two, [[tri-]] is three and [[poly-|poly-, multi-]] is many. Others deal with time:
> [[pre-|ante-, pre-, pro-]] means before and [[post-]] means after. Finally, [[a-|a-, an-]] means without, as
> in apnea, and [[anti-|anti-, contra-]] means against, as in antibiotic.

## Common suffixes
> Suffixes usually tell you what kind of word you are looking at. Diagnostic suffixes describe a condition:
> [[-itis]] means inflammation, [[-osis]] means an abnormal condition, [[-algia|-algia, -dynia]] means pain,
> [[-megaly]] means enlargement, [[-oma]] means tumor and [[-pathy]] means disease. Blood conditions end in
> [[-emia]]. Procedural suffixes describe what is done: [[-ectomy]] is surgical removal, [[-otomy]] is cutting
> into, and [[-ostomy]] is creating an opening. Repair is [[-plasty]], and stitching is [[-rrhaphy]]. Looking
> inside with an instrument is [[-scopy]], done with a [[-scope]]. A record or picture is a [[-gram]], and the
> process of recording it is [[-graphy]]. Puncturing to remove fluid is [[-centesis]]. Watch the look-alikes:
> [[-rrhage|-rrhage, -rrhagia]] is bursting forth of blood, [[-rrhea]] is a flow or discharge, and [[-rrhexis]]
> is a rupture. A deficiency is [[-penia]], paralysis is [[-plegia]], hardening is [[-sclerosis]] and narrowing
> is [[-stenosis]].

## Plurals and pronunciation
> Medical plurals follow Greek and Latin rules rather than simply adding s. A word ending in -a becomes -ae, so
> one vertebra becomes two vertebrae. A word ending in -is changes to -es, so diagnosis becomes diagnoses. A
> word ending in -um changes to -a, as in bacterium and bacteria, and -us changes to -i, as in bronchus and
> bronchi. Words ending in -ix or -ex change to -ices, so appendix becomes appendices. Pronunciation matters
> because some terms sound almost identical. The [[ilium]] is the large wing of the hip bone, while the
> [[ileum]] is the last part of the small intestine. Mixing them up in a chart would point to a completely
> different part of the body, so always check the spelling.
P -a to -ae = singular ending that becomes -ae in the plural, as vertebra to vertebrae
P -is to -es = singular ending that becomes -es in the plural, as diagnosis to diagnoses
P -um to -a = singular ending that becomes -a in the plural, as bacterium to bacteria
P -us to -i = singular ending that becomes -i in the plural, as bronchus to bronchi
P -ix, -ex to -ices = singular endings that become -ices in the plural, as appendix to appendices
P -on to -a = singular ending that becomes -a in the plural, as ganglion to ganglia
P -ma to -mata = singular ending that becomes -mata in the plural, as stoma to stomata
P -nx to -nges = singular ending that becomes -nges in the plural, as phalanx to phalanges
T ilium = the large upper wing-shaped bone of the pelvis; sounds like ileum
T ileum = the last section of the small intestine; sounds like ilium

## The health care team
> Patients are cared for by a team. A [[physician]] holds an MD or DO degree and diagnoses and treats disease. A
> [[physician assistant]] practices medicine as part of a physician-led team, and a [[nurse practitioner]] is an
> advanced practice nurse who can also diagnose and prescribe. A [[registered nurse]] plans and delivers bedside
> care, and a [[medical assistant]] handles both clinical and office tasks. The [[pharmacist]] prepares and
> dispenses medicines. Specialists are named with -logist, -ist or -ician: a [[pediatrician]] treats children, a
> [[geriatrician]] treats older adults, a [[dermatologist]] treats skin, a [[cardiologist]] treats the heart and
> an [[orthopedist]] treats bones and joints. The whole team shares information through the [[electronic health
> record]], and [[HIPAA]] is the law that protects the privacy of that information.
T physician = a licensed medical doctor (MD or DO) who diagnoses and treats disease
T physician assistant = a licensed clinician who practices medicine as part of a physician-led team
T nurse practitioner = an advanced practice registered nurse who can diagnose, treat and prescribe
T registered nurse = a licensed nurse who plans and delivers patient care
T medical assistant = a team member who performs clinical and administrative tasks in a medical office
T pharmacist = a licensed professional who prepares and dispenses medications and advises on their use
T pediatrician = a physician specializing in the care of children | ped/o + iatr/o + -ician
T geriatrician = a physician specializing in the care of older adults | ger/o + iatr/o + -ician
T dermatologist = a specialist in diseases of the skin | dermat/o + -logist
T cardiologist = a specialist in diseases of the heart | cardi/o + -logist
T orthopedist = a specialist in disorders of bones, joints and muscles | orth/o + ped/o + -ist
T electronic health record = a digital record of a patient's health information shared by providers
T HIPAA = the US law that protects the privacy and security of patient health information
